Why I build robots

Short version: I want robots doing the jobs that put people in danger, and I want to be one of the people building them.

Fukushima in 2011 is what got me into this. When those reactors went down, nobody had a robot that could walk in and do the work. Turn a valve, open a door, get through a building made for people. The DARPA Robotics Challenge came out of that, with teams building robots to go where a person could get killed. I was a kid watching it, and it stuck.

I've been working toward that ever since. I was programming lead on FIRST Robotics Team 4514, was part of UMD BattleBots, and wrote perception code for Qubo, the underwater robot Robotics@Maryland takes to competition. Then came two autonomy internships. At Forterra I built the pipeline that keeps tabs on a fleet of autonomous yard vehicles. At KBR I worked on a rover that tracks a person and steers to them, plus a voice assistant that runs entirely on the robot.

Who it's for

What I care about most is who the robot ends up helping. I want what I build to make everyday people's lives better. Medical triage, search and rescue, and taking dangerous work off people's plates are the problems I keep coming back to. The KBR rover was built for that kind of moment: reach someone who's hurt and find out what they need before a responder gets there.

How I work

Build it, throw it at a benchmark, break it, go again. Right now that loop is running on a desktop robot arm, where I'm learning to train policies from demonstrations instead of hand-tuning every controller. I'd rather post an honest success rate than a perfect demo video.

Outside the lab

I lift. Most days I'm in the gym, with running or swimming mixed in for cardio, and I treat training a lot like engineering: track the numbers, change one thing, see what happens. I also have a 3D printer at home for parts, and I speak conversational German.
